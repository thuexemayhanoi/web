Long Bien Bridge Motorbike Riding Guide

Of all the structures a visitor on a rented motorbike can ride across in Hanoi, none carries the weight of Long Bien Bridge. The old cantilever spans over the Red River have carried trains, carts, bicycles and motorbikes for more than a century, and riding its deck remains one of the city's signature two-wheeled experiences. This guide covers what the bridge means to a rider: the approaches from both sides, the surface and traffic character, and how the crossing stitches the Long Bien district to the historic center.

The bridge is close to home ground for us: our shop at 112 Nguyen Van Cu sits in Long Bien, on the river's east bank, and the bridge is a familiar part of our daily riding territory. It pairs naturally with the Old Quarter motorbike rental hub, since the southern approach begins within reach of the Old Quarter streets, and with the West Lake ride for riders building a loop of the city's water landmarks.

What the bridge is and why it matters

Long Bien Bridge was built around the turn of the twentieth century under the French administration and opened in 1902, and it has been repaired many times since, including after damage in past wars. Its steel spans still carry a rail line on the central track, with lanes alongside for two-wheeled traffic and pedestrians. Heavy four-wheeled traffic largely uses newer crossings nearby, which is exactly why the bridge remains such a pleasure for motorbikes: the big vehicles are elsewhere, and the deck belongs to riders, cyclists and walkers.

For the city, the bridge is a working piece of history rather than a museum piece. Trains still cross it, motorbikes still commute over it daily, and the riverbank districts on both sides still orient themselves around it. For a visitor, it offers something almost nowhere else in Hanoi does: a slow, elevated, unobstructed view over the Red River, the island farmland beneath the spans, and the skyline of the city behind you.

The honest framing for riders is that the bridge is an experience crossing rather than a fast route. If the goal is simply to get from the center to Long Bien quickly, the newer bridges serve that purpose. If the goal is to feel the city, the river and a century of Hanoi riding in ten unhurried minutes, Long Bien Bridge is the correct choice.

The southern approach: from the center

From the center of Hanoi, the ride toward the bridge threads through some of the oldest streets in the city. Leaving the Old Quarter's grid, the route crosses the rail lines and industrial streets of the Pho Co Hang Co area before reaching the bridge's southern ramp. The approach streets are a mix of narrow commercial lanes and wider roads, and the volume of motorbike traffic builds steadily the closer you get to the river, because the deck is a genuine commuter artery.

The southern approach rewards a rider's patience more than their speed. The intersections near the bridge mouth are busy, weaving is constant, and the ramp onto the deck narrows the traffic into a stream. The practical advice is to position yourself early, ride at the speed of the stream rather than against it, and use the approach to warm up your attention for the crossing itself.

Navigation is straightforward: from the Old Quarter, head for the streets that lead to the river, and the bridge is unmistakable. The steelwork appears above the rooftops well before you reach it, and on most days the queue of bikes on the ramp confirms the way before any signpost does.

The Long Bien side: our home district

On the east bank, the bridge lands in Long Bien, the district we call home. The contrast with the southern side is immediate: wider roads, newer development, an open sky, and the riverbank fruit-and-vegetable market territory that has grown up around the bridge approaches over generations. Long Bien is where many of the city's riders live and work, and the streets around the bridge mouth carry the ordinary, unglamorous riding traffic of daily life.

For visitors collecting a bike from us at 112 Nguyen Van Cu, the bridge is effectively the front door to the old city: cross it and the Old Quarter, the lakes and the rest of central Hanoi open up within minutes. This is why we recommend practicing a little in Long Bien's calmer streets first, then crossing to the center once the bike feels familiar, rather than beginning the day in the Old Quarter's most demanding lanes.

The riverbank under and around the bridge is worth a slow pass in its own right. The island in the river beside the bridge is farmed, and the views across the water to the fields are a reminder of how quickly Hanoi transitions from metropolis to countryside. Ride the approach roads along the bank, park where local bikes park, and take the time the view has earned.

Riding the deck: surface and traffic

The deck itself is a shared-lane experience. Motorbikes, bicycles, electric bikes and pedestrians share the lanes beside the rail track, with the flow on each side of the bridge organized by convention and habit rather than much in the way of physical separation. The lanes are narrow enough that two bikes pass each other with a conscious decision, and the etiquette is what you would expect: modest speed, steady lines, and no overtaking that requires anyone else to flinch.

The surface deserves honest words. The deck has been patched and repatched across its life, and a rider notices: seams, rivets, plates and the occasional section that is rougher than it looks. The correct response is lower speed and a loose grip rather than tension, letting the bike handle the texture instead of fighting every seam. Riders on scooters with smaller wheels should be a touch more careful than those on larger machines at the rougher spots.

Watch for the rail section and any related works or closures on the day, since maintenance and train movements can reshape the crossing arrangement temporarily. Keep a steady pace, keep to the bike lanes, and give pedestrians the widest line the lane allows, particularly at the viewing spots where people stop for photographs. A bell or a gentle horn well before you reach a stopped group is the polite signal that works.

The best hours to cross

The bridge's character changes by the hour, and timing shapes the experience more than anything else a rider controls. Early morning is the celebrated window: the river mists, the sunrise over the far bank, and the fruit market activity around the approaches give the crossing its most atmospheric mood. Riders who cross at dawn often describe it as the best half hour of their trip, and the photograph they take from the middle of the deck usually supports the claim.

The commuter peaks are the hours to treat with respect. Morning and late afternoon, the deck carries a dense stream of working riders, and the crossing becomes a flowing negotiation rather than a sightseeing lane. It is still perfectly ridable, but the margins are thinner, the photography stops, and the appropriate posture is commute rather than cruise.

Evening and after dark are quieter, and the city lights over the water have their own following. The same night-riding caution applies on the deck as anywhere: unlit stretches, stopped pedestrians, and a surface that rewards modest speed. Whether dawn or dusk, pick one atmospheric crossing, ride it slowly, and treat the utilitarian crossings at other hours as ordinary transport.

Making it part of a loop

The bridge sits naturally inside several of Hanoi's classic two-wheeled loops. The straightforward option is the out-and-back: from the center, across the deck, a slow pass of the Long Bien riverbank, and back across to the Old Quarter for coffee. It takes a relaxed morning and samples everything the crossing offers.

Longer loops connect the bridge to the city's other water landmarks. Riders can cross Long Bien, ride the east bank northward, and return over one of the newer bridges to link up with the West Lake circuit, pairing two of the city's signature rides in one morning. The motorbike guides hub collects the habits that make these longer loops safe and enjoyable, from pre-ride checks to weather judgment, and the West Lake guide covers the northern half of the circuit.

The bridge also marks the start of the road out of the city toward the northern provinces, which makes it a fitting first landmark for riders planning longer trips from Hanoi. Cross it early, watch the farmland appear as the city releases you, and the psychology of the longer ride begins exactly there, at the middle of the Red River.

Frequently asked questions about riding Long Bien Bridge

Can motorbikes cross Long Bien Bridge?

Yes. Motorbikes, bicycles and pedestrians use the lanes alongside the rail track on the historic deck, while most heavy vehicle traffic uses newer crossings nearby. Ride at modest speed, keep to the bike lanes, and give pedestrians room at the viewing spots.

What is the best time of day to ride across?

Early morning is the celebrated window, with river mists and sunrise over the far bank, and the market activity around the approaches adds to the atmosphere. Avoid the commuter peaks for sightseeing, when the deck carries a dense stream of working riders.

Is the bridge surface difficult for a small scooter?

The deck is patched and textured in places, so ride slowly with a loose grip rather than fighting the seams. Small-wheeled scooters should take the rougher spots a touch more carefully than larger bikes, but every bike in our fleet crosses it comfortably at a modest pace.

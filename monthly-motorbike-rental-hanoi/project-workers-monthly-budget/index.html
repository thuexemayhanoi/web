What the Project Budget Contains

The transport budget has three honest layers, and the first mistake most budgets make is counting only the first. The rental line is the visible one, 900,000 to 1,200,000 VND per month for the budget tier of Honda Wave, Yamaha Sirius, Yamaha Mio and Honda Click, and 1,500,000 to 2,000,000 VND for the premium of Honda Vision, Honda Air Blade and the electric motorbike, and it is the number that gets compared, remembered and expensed. The second layer is fuel, which for a working corridor is a weekly bill of small receipts rather than a monthly one, and the third is the loose change of riding, parking where it is charged, the season’s gear, the small costs the machine’s own month generates, each item tiny and the total real.

The honest way to build the budget is all three layers at once, on paper, before the rental is signed, because the model choice itself depends on the total rather than the headline, and a premium whose comfort is worth a million dong a month may still be the wrong line if the second and third layers push the project’s whole transport spend past what the allowance comfortably carries. The arithmetic is a professional’s ordinary work, twenty minutes with the scope documents open, and it is the difference between a transport line that closes monthly and one that explains itself weekly.

The mistake is the headline budget, the rental counted and the riding assumed free, the fuel and the parking discovered one receipt at a time until the month’s quiet surprise is a transport line double what was planned, and the explaining done at the worst possible moment, the project’s own review. The city is affordable and the machine is cheap to run by any global standard, and the affordability is only honest when it is counted honestly.

Fuel on a Working Corridor

Fuel is the budget’s quiet second bill, and a working corridor makes it a predictable one, the same route at the same hours consuming the same amounts, the petrol stations selling at posted prices that do not negotiate, and the honest method being the project’s own, track the first fortnight, total the receipts, and let the rest of the scope ride on evidence instead of estimates. A light scooter on an urban commute sips rather than drinks, and a longer arterial or bridge-crossing route drinks a little more, and both totals are small by the standards of any project’s home currency, but only one of them is the total the budget should carry, which is the real one.

The desk can add a local’s sense of what a typical working month of commuting costs in fuel for each tier, and that sense is a better starting estimate than any page’s guess, though the first fortnight’s own receipts remain the honest final answer. The electric option changes the line entirely, no fuel receipts at all and a charging habit instead, and whether that suits the accommodation, the site and the allowance’s own rules is a briefing question with real answers at the counter, worth having before the month is chosen rather than after.

The mistake is treating fuel as weather, something that happens rather than something that is counted, and then reading the month’s petrol total as a grievance rather than a line item. Fuel is the most countable cost in the whole budget, it accumulates in small visible amounts on the same route daily, and the rider who counts it once never has to be surprised by it again.

Site and Home Parking

Parking in Hanoi appears in two forms, the free kerbside kind and the watched kind that charges a small posted fee for keeping an eye on things, and a project’s week usually meets both, the site’s own arrangements on the working days and the city’s attended lots at the markets, malls and civic addresses the off-hours visit. What the site itself offers is a first-week question asked of the site manager or a colleague, and the answer converts the daily arrival from a hunt into a habit, while the watched lots in the city post their fees openly and charge them without ceremony, and the honest budget holds a small monthly allowance for the sum of all of it.

The allowance is a container rather than a commitment, set from the first week’s real pattern and adjusted once at the month’s turn, and a container that turns out too large is a pleasant discovery while one too small is a leak. The desk at 112 Nguyen Van Cu can add its own local sense of which kinds of addresses tend to charge and which tend not to, and between that and the site’s own answer the line settles inside the first fortnight, which is the correct speed for a line item.

The mistake is the assumption that a city of two-wheelers parks for free by right, which is half true and therefore the dangerous kind of true, the false half appearing as a handful of small fees at exactly the addresses the project visits most. Ask in week one, allow the small line, and the parking question becomes boring, which is the correct thing for a budget line to be.

The Loose-Change Layer

The third layer is the loose change, and it deserves respect precisely because no single item in it does, the rain cape bought in the season’s first serious downpour, the phone mount that ends the pocket-fishing at every junction, the spare poncho because the first was lent to a colleague, the small cover for the storm nights, the occasional coin at a watched lot, and the chain of tiny purchases that a riding month honestly generates. None of it individually worth a thought, all of it collectively worth a line, and the honest line is an allowance set once and spent without ceremony, the layer’s whole cost being small and its whole danger being the moralising that either denies it or inflates it.

The layer also contains the machine’s own small needs between services, the top-up, the bulb, whatever the month brings, and the honest division of those costs between the shop and the renter is desk policy rather than guesswork, stated at signing at 112 Nguyen Van Cu and honoured through the scope. The monthly rental structure exists to absorb the machine’s ordinary life, and the rider who asked the division question on day one carries the answer for the whole project instead of discovering it one evening at a time.

The mistake is funding the layer by denial until the typhoon-eve price of the only rain cape left in the district makes the point, or defending each purchase on its own merits until the total arrives like a stranger. Set the allowance, spend the allowance, close the layer once a month, and it stays as small as it actually is.

The arithmetic matters most at the short end, because a three-week scope is a different calculation from a six-month one, the weekly rate meeting the monthly at a crossover the budget should know in advance, and the honest answer is that anything past a few weeks leans monthly and anything under it leans weekly, with the ground between being exactly the ground the desk covers best. The mistake is leaving the edges to goodwill, the partial weeks assumed generous because the handover was warm, the numbers never asked because the question felt small, and the demobilisation week turning into the project’s last negotiation instead of its quiet conclusion.

Two Anchors, A Hundred Standings

The commuter’s security geography is a two-point map, the place the machine sleeps and the place it waits through the working day, and the planning hour treats both as real estate decisions rather than afterthoughts, because a month multiplies whatever each standing point offers, a watched lot becoming a hundred watched evenings and an unwatched kerb becoming a hundred quiet risks. The honest plan starts by walking both addresses the way a surveyor would, noting the watched ground, the posted arrangements, the flow of people at the hours the machine actually stands, and letting the month’s repetition turn each small difference into a large one.

The repetition also changes what counts as convenient, the spot that saves thirty seconds of walking but sits beyond every watching eye losing to the spot that costs a minute and sits under a shop’s whole attention, because the commuter pays for a bad spot a hundred times while paying for a good one exactly once, in the planning hour. The frequent rider’s advantage is knowledge, a month on the same streets teaches which corners empty after dark and which lots never do, and the plan’s honest form is to use that knowledge rather than fight it.

The mistake is parking the way the tourist parks, wherever the morning finds a gap, when the commuter’s standings are appointments rather than improvisations, and the machine’s month is spent mostly at the two addresses the plan either secured or ignored. Plan the two anchors first. The hundred standings will follow the plan.

The Home End, The Night Question

The night is the longer standing, and the home end’s honest question is where the machine actually sleeps, indoors in a building’s own arrangement, in a courtyard the household watches, on a kerb the street’s life keeps honest, or in a gap that nobody’s eyes ever cross, and the answer is worth knowing before the rental month begins rather than after the first uneasy week. Buildings vary, some with their own parking layer and its posted terms, some with nothing but the street, and the resident who asks the building and the neighbours first has designed the night honestly.

The night also has a ritual shape, the machine returned to its anchor the same way each evening, locked through whatever the anchor offers, the cover on if the season wants one, and the helmet carried inside rather than left to balance, a sequence small enough to feel superstitious and reliable enough that its absence is noticed. The ritual’s real work is attention, a machine checked nightly is a machine whose changes are noticed nightly, and the commuter’s hundred nights reward any habit that pays attention on schedule.

The mistake is treating the home end as solved because the machine is near the door, when nearness is not watching and the street’s honest answer to the night question varies block by block. Ask the building, ask the neighbours, and let the machine’s longest standing happen on ground that somebody actually sees.

The mistake is choosing the work end by the morning’s hurry alone, the nearest gap taken and the lot three doors down never inspected, when the difference between the two is measured over a month of identical mornings.

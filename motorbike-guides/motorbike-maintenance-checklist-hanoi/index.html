Motorbike Maintenance Checklist for Hanoi Renters

You do not need to be a mechanic to ride well in Hanoi, but you do need a short list of checks that takes two minutes and catches almost everything before it becomes a problem. This maintenance checklist is written for renters, not owners: it covers what to look at before you ride, what deserves a message to us, and how to treat the bike so it treats you well in return.

The bikes in our fleet are maintained by us, which is our job, and the checks below are the renter's share of the partnership: light, quick and worth every minute. This page belongs to the practical topics collected in the motorbike guides hub.

The two-minute pre-ride routine

The routine is deliberately small, because a checklist nobody runs is worthless. Walk a slow circle around the bike. Look at both tires for damage and obvious softness. Squeeze both brake levers and feel for firmness. Check that the lights work, front and back, and beep the horn once. Glance at the fuel gauge or confirm the battery level on electric models. Make sure the mirrors are set for you, not for the previous rider.

Run it before the first ride of the day, and in a shorter version before any ride where the bike has been parked somewhere new. It takes about two minutes, it needs no tools and no knowledge beyond your own senses, and it catches the vast majority of small issues while they are still small. Riders who make it a habit stop noticing they do it, which is the goal of every good routine.

The deeper inspection at pickup is a different exercise and it happens once, with us, before money changes hands. That process, described in how to rent a motorbike in Hanoi, includes photographing existing scratches and having them acknowledged, so the return conversation stays short and friendly. The daily routine below protects your ride; the pickup inspection protects your deposit conversation.

Tires: pressure and damage

Tires are the only parts of the bike that touch the road, which makes them the most important two minutes of the checklist. Look at the tread face for anything embedded, any cuts, any bubbles in the sidewall. Press your palm hard against the contact patch and rock the bike slightly to feel whether the tire deflects like a reasonably firm cushion or squashes like a tired one. You are not measuring pressure with instruments; you are comparing against every bike tire you have ever leaned on, and your hands are better at this than you expect.

What you are looking for changes over a rental period. A tire that felt fine on Monday and feels soft on Thursday is a message, not a mood. Slow leaks announce themselves exactly this way, and a two-minute check is how they announce themselves in a parking lot instead of on a bridge. Wet-season riders get an extra reason for attention, because tire grip is what rain riding spends first, and the habit of glancing at tread is the cheapest wet-weather insurance there is.

If a tire looks or feels wrong, do not ride it to see whether it stays wrong. Flag it to us the same day, and either we reassure you or we swap the bike. This is exactly the kind of thing we want to hear about early, because a tire top-up or replacement is quick and drama-free, while a tire failure in traffic is neither.

Brakes: feel beats theory

The brake check is a feel test, and your hands already know how to run it. Squeeze the front lever and the rear pedal, or both levers on scooters, and pay attention to three things: firmness at the lever, resistance that builds smoothly, and a lever that returns freely when you release it. Brakes should feel confident and consistent, not spongy, not loose, and not different from one squeeze to the next.

During your first slow loop of the day, test both brakes gently at low speed where it is safe. This is the habit that turns a static check into a real one, because brakes that feel fine at the curb can still behave differently under actual load, and a gentle low-speed test tells you so without drama. The same two-second test works for riders of every level, and it is worth doing after any day the bike has sat unused.

A brake that feels soft, that pulls to one side, or that makes a noise you have not heard before is a same-day flag to us. Brakes are the one system where our patience for early reports is infinite, because brake problems are cheap to fix early and expensive, in every currency, to discover late. Never ride a bike whose brakes have told you something and hope the message changes.

Lights, horn and electronics

The lights and horn are how you are seen and heard in traffic, which in Hanoi is most of your safety system. Run the headlight on high and low, check the tail light and the brake light, and press the horn once, politely, toward the street. On automatic scooters and electric models, the electronics extend to the dash: confirm the display reads sensibly, and on electric bikes confirm the battery indicator matches what you expect for your day's plan.

Horn deserves a special word, because visitors sometimes feel rude using it. In Vietnamese traffic the horn is communication, not aggression: a short beep says I am here, and it is used constantly, lightly and without hostility by everyone. A horn that works is a real safety feature here, which is why it sits on the daily checklist, and a rider who uses it early and gently is riding the local way.

Electrical gremlins on a rental are rare, but the check costs seconds. A light that flickers, a dash that resets, an indicator that sticks: all are flags, and all are quicker to fix than the confusion they cause if they fail during a night ride. The FAQ hub answers the common rental questions, and specific questions about the bike you are riding are always welcome directly with us.

Fuel and charge levels

The fuel check is planning, not maintenance, but it earns its place on the list because it is the one item that strands people. Glance at the gauge before you ride, and know roughly what the day's plan demands. Inside Hanoi, fuel stations are dense enough that planning is barely necessary, but a rider heading out of the city for a day trip should start with a full tank and know that the return leg is covered.

On electric models, the equivalent habit is charge awareness: confirm the battery level against the day's expected distance, and keep the overnight-charging routine described in our electric rental guide. Electricity and fuel share the same rule, which is that the best time to discover a shortage is in the morning at your accommodation, not at the far end of your route.

Both habits are described more fully elsewhere in our guides, including the fueling practices and etiquette covered on the fuel guide, but the checklist version is simple: know your level before you leave, and top up before you need to rather than when you have to.

What to flag to us, and when

The rule of thumb for renters is this: anything that changes, tell us; anything that worries you, ask us. The list of things worth a message includes soft brakes, soft or damaged tires, lights or horn failures, new noises, new vibrations, and anything electrical behaving oddly. The list of things not worth a message is much shorter, and mostly consists of cosmetic details that were photographed at pickup.

Timing matters more than detail. A flag sent the day you notice something is almost always a small repair or a quick swap; the same problem discovered at return, or ignored for a week, can turn a rental into an unpleasant conversation. Renters who communicate early and matter-of-factly are the renters we can best look after, and the habit costs one message.

Equally important is what not to attempt. Renters should not perform repairs themselves, adjust what they do not understand, or take the bike to a third-party workshop without talking to us first. Not because of rules for their own sake, but because a bike you are riding is our responsibility, and a problem we hear about is one we can actually solve, quickly and at no drama to you.

Living with the bike between checks

Between checks, the renter's job is respect, and respect on two wheels is mostly parking. Park where parking is expected, use attended strips and lots, take your belongings with you, and do not force the bike into spaces that scrape or strain it. A bike parked well is a bike that starts, rides and returns without stories, and most of the small damage rentals accumulate comes from parking optimism rather than riding.

The other habit is weather sense. In the rainy season, a bike left under sky collects water in places that cause no harm but cause small mysteries, so a moment of shelter for the bike is a kindness you both benefit from. In the dry season, shade protects the seat and your legs equally. None of this is maintenance in the technical sense, and all of it shows up in how the bike treats your remaining days with it.

That is the whole contract, and it is deliberately light: two minutes of checks, early flags when something changes, and gentle habits between. Do that, and the machine disappears into the background of your trip, which is the correct place for transport, and the sign of a rental that ends exactly the way every good one does: with a short goodbye and a bike that worked the whole time.

Frequently asked questions about rental maintenance

Do I need to do any maintenance on a rental motorbike?

Servicing is our job. Your job is the two-minute pre-ride routine: tires, brakes, lights, horn, fuel or charge, mirrors. Anything that changes or worries you, tell us the same day.

What should I do if the bike develops a problem mid-rental?

Stop riding it if it feels unsafe, and contact us right away. Most issues are quick fixes or a bike swap, and early reports are always easier to solve than late ones.

Can I take the rental bike to a workshop myself?

Talk to us first. The bike is our responsibility during your rental, and problems we hear about are problems we can solve quickly, often without any cost or delay to your plans.

The Yen Tu route asks the machine for three temperaments in one day. The fringe asks for obedience, the instant stops and instant decisions of Hanoi’s opening argument, where a machine that hesitates is a machine the traffic will educate. The corridor asks for stability and presence, the ability to hold a line beside commerce that cannot stop quickly and to show its lights honestly in every grey hour. The approach asks for humility, a quiet, patient machine for the pilgrimage’s last lanes, where the machine’s manners are part of the visit’s respect.

What the route does not ask for is mountain muscle, because Yen Tu’s vertical hours belong to feet, and the machine’s job at the mountain is to wait well, parked properly, out of the pilgrimage’s way, and to carry its rider home with the day’s ending still intact. The choice, then, is a distance-and-manners verdict rather than a power verdict, and the desk’s fleet briefs are written in exactly that language, which is why the counter conversation beats any specification sheet.

The mistake is choosing for the mountain’s drama, because the mountain never meets the machine, and the corridor meets it all day.

What the Yamaha Sirius is

The Sirius is Yamaha's underbone bike for the same job the Honda Wave does: cheap, simple, everyday transport that survives years of hard use. It has a foot-operated gear lever and no hand clutch, a flat seat, a rack and a fuel-efficient small engine. You see them all over Hanoi carrying deliveries, market goods and commuters.

For a renter, that DNA translates into three things. The bike is light enough to push and park easily. The parts and service network around it is enormous, because half the city rides similar machines. And the riding position is upright and natural, with a seat height most riders manage without trouble. None of this is glamorous, and that is exactly the point of the category.

If you are new to Vietnam's bike categories, the choice is simpler than the model names suggest: semi-automatic underbones like the Sirius and Wave, automatic scooters like the scooters in our fleet, and the light 50cc machines covered in the 50cc motorbike rental guide.

Sirius versus Honda Wave

The Sirius and the Wave rent for identical verified rates, so the comparison is character, not cost. Riders who try both usually describe the difference as feel: the Wave is the default choice with the largest installed base, while the Sirius is the alternative for riders who prefer Yamaha's styling or simply like the way it sits.

Functionally the bikes are interchangeable. Both are semi-automatic underbones, both carry the same tier of weekly and monthly pricing, and both are appropriate for beginners who accept shifting gears with their left foot. When both are available, sit on each and pick the one that feels right; when one is taken, the other loses you nothing. That is rare in a price list, and worth using: at 150,000 VND per day, the semi-automatic tier undercuts the automatic Honda Click and Yamaha Mio at the same price while offering more traditional bikes.

Semi-automatic riding for newcomers

Riding the Sirius means managing four gears with your left foot and nothing with your left hand. The pattern is simple: ease off the throttle, press the lever down for higher gears, up for lower, and reapply the throttle. There is no clutch to slip and no way to stall in the usual sense, which is why semi-automatics are considered beginner-friendly in Vietnam despite the gears.

The learning curve is an afternoon, not a week. Start on quiet streets, practice shifting up and down through the full range, and add traffic once the pattern is automatic in your feet. Two habits from local riders are worth copying: shift early rather than revving high, and use both brakes together smoothly. Practical life with a Sirius

The Sirius is happiest doing everyday jobs: commuting, errands, and paved day trips out of the city. Fuel costs stay modest on a small underbone, and fuel stations are dense across Hanoi, so planning around fuel is rarely necessary inside the city. Storage is a rack rather than a trunk, so plan for a backpack or a bungee cord rather than under-seat space.

Where the Sirius earns its keep is distance-per-dong on longer stays. A month on the cheapest tier keeps transport costs minimal, and the bike's simplicity means less to go wrong. Riders planning routes out of the city can start from the Hanoi motorbike trips hub for ideas and planning advice.

The Sirius in Hanoi life: routes and reality

It helps to picture what a week with the Sirius actually looks like. Day one is pickup and the first careful loops around Long Bien. Day two, the ride into the center becomes routine: across the bridge or down the dyke road, into the Old Quarter's orbit, and a first try at the pavement parking strips. By day three, the gears are muscle memory and the bike has become the default answer to every question the city asks, from where to eat to how to reach a museum before it closes.

That is the pattern for every bike category, but the Sirius gets there with the least ceremony. There is nothing to configure, nothing to be careful with, and nothing to learn beyond the foot lever. Renters who were nervous about riding in Vietnam consistently report the same thing by mid-week: the traffic reads as chaotic from a sidewalk and as a rhythm from a seat, and a simple bike lets you concentrate entirely on that rhythm.

The Sirius also shrinks the map. Distances that feel long on foot, such as the stretch between the Old Quarter and the Long Bien side of the river, are minutes on an underbone, and the ring around West Lake becomes a normal evening option rather than an excursion. Riders who keep the bike for a month end up reorganizing their whole stay around the mobility, which is the strongest argument for the monthly rates over stacking days.

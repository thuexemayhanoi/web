The Visitor’s Inspection, Slower Than The Deal

A long-stay tourist buying a used motorbike in Hanoi is buying at a structural disadvantage that has nothing to do with sellers, because the visitor’s local knowledge is weeks old, the machine’s history is invisible, and the exit is already scheduled. The inspection exists to cancel that disadvantage, and it works only when it is slower than the deal. Sellers of every nationality respect a buyer who reads a machine carefully and distrust one who hurries, so the visitor’s patience is not politeness but armour, and this guide arranges it section by section.

The framework below asks the machine questions in a deliberate order: skeleton first, then the parts that touch the road, then the engine, then the electrics, then the ride, and the papers last, because the papers deserve a rested reader rather than an excited one. It invents nothing: no fake prices, no folklore about models, and where a live fact is needed, weather, paperwork rules, market conditions, it points at checking the day’s truth instead of remembering last year’s. The visitor who follows it will either buy well or walk away well, and both are victories.

The mistake is matching the seller’s pace, because the seller owns the pace and the visitor owns the consequences.

Reading The Frame Like A Return Ticket

Sight down the machine before touching anything. The frame should be straight in the way a skeleton is straight, visibly, from every angle, with no creases, no amateur welds, no repainted patches that do not match their neighbours. Scuffs on the bar ends, levers and pegs are the machine’s diary of falls, ordinary in city life, but their story should be consistent: light scuffs say the machine was dropped standing; heavy ones say it met the road at speed, and the difference matters more than any polish.

For a visitor, the frame has a second meaning: it is the part that decides whether the resale at the end of the stay is a conversation or an apology. Plastic is cheap to replace and easy to flatter; a bent frame is neither, and no price compensates for it. Walk around the machine twice, once for the metal and once for the story, and where the two disagree, believe the metal. A used machine is allowed a history in Hanoi; it is not allowed a concealed one, and the frame is where concealment runs out of places to hide.

The mistake is forgiving the skeleton because the paint looks new, because paint is the cheapest virtue in the secondhand world.

Where The Rubber Meets The Reader

The wheels and brakes are where the inspection stops being about history and starts being about next month. Tires deserve a close read: cracked sidewalls, flattened profiles and wear bars showing are bills with dates attached, and on a visitor’s short horizon they are bills the visitor will pay personally. Spin each wheel and watch the rim for hops and dents, and check the spokes or casting for damage that a wash cannot hide. Then the brakes: look at the pads through the caliper or drum, squeeze the levers and press the pedal, and note whether the bite is early and firm or spongy and late.

The chain and sprockets complete the running gear’s testimony. Rust that wipes away is cosmetic; rust that stays, links that kink and teeth worn into waves are the final drive announcing its retirement plans, and a seller who ignored the chain has ignored its oil. For a long-stay tourist the economics are sharper than for a resident: every worn part is not just a cost but a cost inside a fixed window, money spent on repairs that was budgeted for the country. The machine should start its visit already honest.

The mistake is grading the running gear on photographs, because wear reads clearly in person and flatters itself in pictures.

Cold Starts And Honest Exhausts

The engine’s most truthful minute is its first one, so arrange to hear it cold, before the seller has had a chance to warm it into good manners. A healthy machine wakes promptly, settles into an idle that does not wander, and does not need a throttle ceremony to stay awake. Watch the exhaust while it runs: blue smoke says oil is going where it should not, heavy white smoke on a warm day says coolant is visiting the cylinders, and persistent black smoke says the fuelling has opinions. None of these is a verdict; all of them are evidence.

Then listen for the sounds that matter. A light tick at idle can be valve clearance, a knock under load is a bearing with ambitions, and a rattle that changes when the clutch is pulled in narrows the diagnosis. Blip the throttle and feel for revs that rise and fall cleanly rather than hanging or stuttering, and ask about the service history in plain terms, listening for specifics rather than assurances. The visitor’s advantage here is honesty with oneself: nobody is obliged to understand engines, only to refuse to be hurried while one runs.

The mistake is judging the engine after the seller warmed it up, because warmth is the engine’s makeup and cold is its face.

The Switchboard Hour

Electrics are the part of a used machine where neglect shows earliest and cheapest, and an hour with the switches tells the visitor how the machine was really kept. Work the list without skipping: headlight in both beams, brake light from both controls, all four indicators, the horn, the instrument cluster, and every switch on the bar. A dead bulb is a trivial repair and a serious signal, because a seller who could not replace a bulb could not be bothered, and machines are kept or neglected as wholes.

Look at the wiring itself. Tape that does not match the factory colours, connectors hanging free and cables resting against the exhaust are the system’s confession that somebody improvised, and improvised electricity matures into intermittent faults that only appear in the rain, at night, far from home. Try the electric start and count the cranks, and remember that a tired battery can impersonate a tired engine on scooters. For a visitor far from a mechanic they have not met yet, the reliability question outranks the beauty question, and the switchboard hour is where it is answered.

The mistake is assuming electrics are trivia, because electrical neglect is the first chapter of every breakdown story.

A Ride That Answers Questions

The test ride is the exam the machine cannot rehearse, and no long-stay tourist should sign for a machine that has not taken one. Agree with the seller on who bears what, wear a helmet, and choose a quiet street where mistakes are cheap. Ask the machine the same questions in order: does it track straight with a light grip, is the steering free of notchiness, does the front stay composed under gentle braking, does the rear feel planted or vague. Stop and start several times, use the mirrors, and end with one firm stop from real speed.

Ride the machine as it will actually be ridden. On a semi-automatic, run every gear and feel for a draggy clutch, a box that jumps out of gear or a selector that needs convincing; on a scooter, feel for shudder on take-off and flat spots in the mid-range. A machine that behaves in front of its owner for ten honest minutes has earned a second conversation, and one that misbehaves in the easiest possible conditions has answered the most important question asked all day. The ride is also the visitor’s only chance to feel whether the machine fits, and fit is not negotiable after purchase.

The mistake is skipping the ride because the itinerary is calling, because itineraries forget and faults move in permanently.

Papers In A Second Language

For a visitor, the paperwork is the inspection’s final and most foreign section, and it deserves the slowest reading of all. The registration paper should exist in the buyer’s hands, its identifying numbers should match the numbers stamped on the frame and engine, and the name on it should belong to the seller or come with a documented explanation rather than a charming one. Every rule about transfers, foreign ownership and requirements belongs to current official sources that change on their own schedule, so the visitor’s job is to know which questions to ask and where the answers live, not to remember answers at all.

Bring help without outsourcing judgment. A friend or acquaintance who reads Vietnamese can translate what the paper says, and the desk at 112 Nguyen Van Cu can explain what a registration paper should look like, what buyers of machines tend to ask, and which requirements need verification where, as a shop that rents and never sells and therefore has no stake in any purchase. Photograph everything, sleep on any deal that demands same-day money, and treat urgency from a seller as information about the seller. The exit at the end of the stay begins with the papers at the beginning.

The mistake is trusting charm in a second language, because charm is exactly what lost papers wear.

Slowly, and in this order: frame and bodywork first, read for straightness and consistent damage; then the running gear, tires, brakes, rims, chain and sprockets; then a cold start with a careful listen and a look at the exhaust; then every electrical switch on the bar; then a real test ride on a quiet street; and finally the registration paper, checked number against number and translated properly. What should a visitor check most carefully on a used motorbike before buying in Hanoi?

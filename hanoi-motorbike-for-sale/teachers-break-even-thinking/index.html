Teachers: Break-Even Thinking for Hanoi Motorbikes

The break-even question for a teacher in Hanoi, how many months until a bought motorbike costs less than a rented one, is asked with hope and answered honestly only with the school calendar in hand. The teacher's case is not the nomad's and not the expat lifer's: it has a fixed shape, terms, holidays and a departure that is scheduled years in advance, and the calculation belongs to that shape. This guide builds the honest method, invents no universal number, and prices no used machines, comparing instead against verified rental figures and the teacher's own calendar.

The briefing and the verified figures come from Motorbike Rental - Nguyen Tu, 112 Nguyen Van Cu in Long Bien, Hanoi, open daily 09:00 to 21:00, where the rent-versus-buy calculation is run with teachers every August without a thumb on the scale, because the shop rents machines and never sells them. No Universal Number, Only Your Calendar

The staffroom contains confident break-even numbers, three months, six months, a year, and they are all different because they were computed for different lives. The honest version uses the teacher's own inputs: the contract's honest length, the holidays' honest geography, the commute's honest demands, and the exit's honest date, and it produces an answer that is correct for one teacher and wrong for their neighbour. This guide builds the method, not the number.

What can be said universally is structural: rental costs are flat, front-predictable and exit-free, ownership costs are lumpy, back-loaded and exit-priced, and the two lines cross where the calendar says they cross, not where enthusiasm wishes.

There is also no universal break-even because the inputs are not shared between two teachers even at the same school. One lives a ten-minute walk from the gate and rides twice a week; another commutes across the bridge daily and rides all weekend. The first owns a hobby if they buy, the second owns a tool, and the same purchase price behaves completely differently in the two budgets. The calculation that matters is therefore not the generic one that staffroom debates keep re-running, but the personal one, built from the actual contract, the actual commute and the actual holiday plans, and it belongs to the only person who knows those three numbers.

The mistake is adopting a colleague's break-even, because their contract, their summer and their luck are all different from yours.

Duration: The Contract As The Input

The first input is the contract, read honestly. A one-year contract with an uncertain renewal is a short-stay case wearing a long-stay's hopes, and the calculation should weight the short end; a settled multi-year posting is the genuine long-stay case where ownership can compete on the numbers. Teachers know the difference between what a contract says and what it means, and the break-even should be fed the meaning.

Duration interacts with the machine's reality: the purchase price amortizes over the months the teacher is actually present, not the months the contract runs, and a machine idle for summers is amortizing over ten months a year while existing for twelve. The honest input is presence, not paperwork.

The rental side of the arithmetic is the easy half, because it is printed in verified figures: Honda Wave, Yamaha Sirius, Yamaha Mio and Honda Click at 150,000 VND per day, 700,000 VND per week and 900,000 to 1,200,000 VND per month, with Honda Vision at 200,000 VND per day, 1,000,000 VND per week and 1,800,000 to 2,000,000 VND per month, and Honda Air Blade and electric machines at 200,000 VND per day, 1,000,000 VND per week and 1,500,000 VND per month. A teacher who multiplies the monthly figure by the months the bike will actually be ridden, rather than the months of the contract, holds the honest rental number, and that single correction, actual riding months, is where most break-even calculations quietly go wrong in ownership's favour.

The mistake is feeding the calculation the contract's dates instead of the teacher's presence, because machines cannot read contracts, only calendars.

The Holiday Line: Machines Do Not Take Summers Off

The long holiday is the teacher's break-even wild card. A machine owned through an away summer continues to cost, storage, aging battery, tires, fuel, and continued theft and parking risk, while producing nothing; the rental equivalent simply pauses. When the honest comparison adds a "summer line" to the ownership ledger, many one-year cases resolve themselves, and the flatness of the rental figures starts to look less like a price and more like a feature.

The holiday line also has a psychological cost the spreadsheet omits: a machine waiting in Hanoi while the teacher is elsewhere is a small administrative worry carried at distance, and teachers carry enough of those. The honest calculation prices the worry, even at a modest rate.

The holiday line deserves its own row because it is the row teachers forget to draw. A machine owned through a three-month summer abroad still ages, still occupies parking, still risks theft, and still needs revival attention on return, while a rental simply does not exist during the same months and bills nothing for them. Add the summer to the ownership side and subtract it from the rental side, and the gap between the two columns widens by exactly the amount of the holiday, every year of the contract. Break-even thinking that skips this row will keep concluding that buying wins, and it will keep being wrong in the specific way that costs teachers the most.

The mistake is pricing the machine by teaching weeks, because the machine bills for every week it exists, and the summer is not a discount.

Hassle Priced In Mornings

The second ledger is hassle, and the teacher's unit of hassle is the morning. Ownership's hassles arrive as flat tires before first period, batteries dead before parents' evening, and workshop visits that must be scheduled around a timetable that does not move; rental's hassles are mostly a phone call and a machine that is either fixed or swapped by people whose whole job is the fleet. The break-even that ignores mornings is a break-even written by someone who has never taught a Monday.

The honest question is what mornings are worth to this teacher, and the honest answer varies: a rider who enjoys machines may price the workshop afternoon as a pleasure, while a rider on a tight schedule prices it as the most expensive hour of the week. Neither is wrong, and the calculation belongs to whoever pays it.

The hassle row is real even when it is hard to price, because it is priced in the currency teachers spend least freely: mornings. An owned machine that needs a workshop visit before a 7:30 class charges the day's most expensive hour, and the same repair on a rental is a five-minute conversation at the desk and a different machine, running, by the afternoon. Over a contract the repair events are roughly equal in probability; what differs is who absorbs the scheduling, the sourcing of parts and the waiting. Renting is, among other things, the purchase of a maintenance department that answers in minutes, and that purchase belongs in the arithmetic rather than in the footnotes where teachers usually file it.

The mistake is converting everything to dong, because dong is the only currency the comparison knows how to flatter.

The Exit: Where Teacher Cases Are Decided

Teachers leave on dates that are known years in advance, and that is the exit's double nature: it is predictable, so the resale can be planned properly, and it is hard, so an unplanned resale is punished severely. The exit cost includes the resale's discount under deadline pressure, the transfer paperwork's schedule, and the attention both demand during the term's last weeks, which are exactly the weeks teachers have least to spare.

Rental's exit is a handshake: the machine returns, the conversation is short, and the departure contains no machines. When the exit is priced honestly, many teacher cases conclude that the rental's flat figures were quietly including an insurance policy against the calendar, and the policy was worth more than its premium.

The mistake is treating the exit as the term's last week, because the exit is a cost that begins on buying day and only invoices at the end.

Frequently asked questions about break-even thinking for teachers in Hanoi

How long until buying a motorbike is cheaper than renting for a teacher in Hanoi?

There is no universal number.
